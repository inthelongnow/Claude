The evidence

What the research says

A plain summary of where neurofeedback research stands, including what is still unsettled. Reviewed against published studies in November 2026.

  • Most people who train improveAcross trials, people who complete neurofeedback usually report meaningful improvement, often large. Where studies followed people up, the gains generally held.
  • Why they improve is debatedIn the most rigorous trials, people given sham feedback often improve about as much. Part of the benefit may come from the whole experience: regular sessions, focused practice, support and expectation.
  • It appears safeLarge trials report no serious harm from training. Reactions are usually mild and short-lived.

Where the research is heading

  • Larger, better-controlled trials, reported to an agreed checklist so that studies can be compared.
  • Rethinking comparison conditions. Sham feedback turns out to be partly active, which makes "real versus sham" harder to interpret than it sounds.
  • Working out who learns and who benefits. Many participants do not show the trained change, and early learning seems to predict the rest.
  • More personalised protocols, guided by a person's own rhythms or brain map.
  • Brain-imaging studies of what changes with training, and well-tested home devices.

What this means for us

I offer neurofeedback as one part of your care, alongside whatever else is helping you, and not in place of treatments with stronger evidence.

We track your own response from the start: your goals, your symptom ratings and what you notice. If training is helping, we will see it. If it is not, we will see that too and change course.
